# Hip Therapy Belt -- Broad Targeting Longform Ad Briefs

## Product: Hip Therapy Belt (Red Light 660nm, Infrared 850nm, Heat, Vibration)
## Market: Australia | Audience: 45-65
## Angles: Non-surgery. Symptom-led, condition-led, identity-led, relationship-led.
## Source: Deep Research & Strategy Document + VOC Research

**Creative Instruction:** The hooks listed in each brief are guides, not fixed. Use them as starting points. Remix, combine, or write new ones that fit the angle. Minimum 3 hook options per brief so the creative team can test variations. Add 2 more if needed. Match the tone -- some are emotional, some are stat-driven, some are confrontational. These briefs target broader audiences who may NOT be considering surgery. They are in pain. They want relief. They don't need to be scared into action -- they need to be shown a path.

# Brief 1: Cortisone Failure

## ICP
Australians 45-65 who have had 1-3 cortisone injections for hip pain and are watching the relief shrink each time. They know cortisone is temporary. They know they can only get 3-4 shots per year. They're looking for something that doesn't wear off in 2 weeks. Many have bursitis or OA. Mostly women (bursitis has 2-5x higher rate in women).

## POV
First person. Woman, 57. Had three cortisone shots in the last 18 months. The first lasted 3 months. The second lasted 6 weeks. The third barely lasted 2 weeks. She's out of shots for the year and the pain is back worse than before.

## Summary of the Story
A woman who trusted cortisone as her go-to for hip pain watches it fail in slow motion. Each injection lasts shorter than the last. Her GP tells her she can't have another one for 3 months. She reads that repeated cortisone can damage cartilage. She realises she's been masking the problem while the problem gets worse. She finds the belt because she needs daily relief that doesn't run out.

## Type
Treatment failure / diminishing returns narrative

## Desire Type
Desire for consistency. She doesn't want another temporary fix. She wants something that works every time she uses it, not something that wears off.

## Awareness
Solution-aware. She knows cortisone exists. She knows physio exists. She's looking for the NEXT thing because the current things have a shelf life. She needs to see that a 4-in-1 belt targets inflammation daily, not once every 3 months.

## Concept
The ad opens with a countdown. "Shot 1: 3 months of relief. Shot 2: 6 weeks. Shot 3: 14 days. Shot 4: My GP said I can't have another one until September." Each shot delivers less. The viewer watches the returns shrink. The pivot: "Cortisone gives you 14 days. This gives you every night." The belt enters as the thing that doesn't run out, doesn't damage cartilage, and doesn't need a referral.

## Angle
Cortisone failure. The temporary fix that's getting less temporary every time.

## Current Belief
- "Cortisone is the best non-surgical option for hip pain."
- "The injection targets the problem directly."
- "It's working -- it just wears off."
- "I'll just keep getting shots when I need them."
- "A belt can't do what an injection does."

## Belief Shift
- FROM: "Cortisone is managing my pain" > TO: "Cortisone is masking my pain while potentially damaging my cartilage. And each shot works less."
- FROM: "Nothing matches an injection" > TO: "Infrared at 850nm reaches the same depth as the injection, targets inflammation daily, and doesn't wear off in 2 weeks."

## Moments
- Sitting in the radiology suite for the third time, thinking "this better last longer than the last one"
- Feeling the pain return 10 days after the injection and thinking "already?"
- Reading online that cortisone can cause avascular necrosis and feeling her stomach drop
- Calling the GP to book another shot and being told "not for another 3 months"
- Lying awake at midnight with the hip burning, knowing there's no injection available

## Hooks
- "Shot 1: 3 months of relief. Shot 2: 6 weeks. Shot 3: 14 days. Shot 4: Denied."
- "Cortisone gave me 14 days. This belt gives me every night."

# Brief 2: Sleep Deprivation

## ICP
Australians 50-70 whose hip pain is destroying their sleep. They can't lie on their side. They wake up at 2am, 3am, 4am. They toss and turn. The pain is manageable during the day but unbearable at night. They're exhausted. Their partner is exhausted. Sleep deprivation is affecting their mood, their energy, their relationships. "I can't sleep on my side" is the #1 complaint across all hip conditions.

## POV
First person. Woman, 62. She hasn't slept through the night in 8 months. She sleeps with a pillow between her knees. She's moved to the spare room so she doesn't wake her husband. The exhaustion is worse than the pain.

## Summary of the Story
A woman who used to sleep like a log now dreads bedtime. Her left hip wakes her up at 3am every night. She's tried every position, every pillow, every mattress topper. She's tried heat packs that go cold by midnight. She's tried paracetamol before bed. Nothing keeps the pain away past 2am. She finds the belt and wears it for 20 minutes before bed. The infrared heat and red light calm the inflammation enough to sleep.

## Type
Night pain / exhaustion spiral

## Desire Type
Desire for rest. Not just sleep -- rest. She wants to close her eyes without bracing for the 3am wake-up. She wants to feel like a person again in the morning.

## Awareness
Problem-aware. She knows she has hip pain. She associates it with daytime difficulty. She hasn't connected it to a treatable condition that specific therapies can address. She's been treating the symptom (sleeplessness) instead of the source (inflammation in the joint).

## Concept
The ad opens at 3am. Dark room. Phone screen glowing. "3:17am. Left hip. Again." The viewer knows this moment. They've lived it. The ad doesn't lecture about red light wavelengths. It talks about sleep. "I wore it for 20 minutes before bed. The heat and infrared calmed the joint. I slept until 6am. First time in 8 months." The belt enters as a sleep solution, not a pain device.

## Angle
Sleep deprivation. The 3am wake-up that's stealing your days, not just your nights.

## Current Belief
- "Hip pain at night is just something I have to live with."
- "I've tried everything -- different pillows, positions, mattresses."
- "Pain relief is for daytime. Night pain is different."
- "A belt won't help me sleep."

## Belief Shift

- FROM: "I need pills to sleep" > TO: "20 minutes of therapy before bed targets the inflammation at the joint. Not a pill. Not a heat pack that goes cold. Consistent, nightly, targeted relief."

- Sitting on the edge of the bed at 5am, too exhausted to get up, too sore to lie back down

# Brief 3: Morning Stiffness

## POV

## Summary of the Story
A man who spent 40 years doing physical work now can't get out of bed without a production. His hip joint stiffens overnight. The first steps feel like his hip is rusted shut. He stands at the kitchen bench, gripping the counter, waiting for his body to catch up with his brain. He finds the belt and wears it for 15 minutes while the kettle boils. ## Type

## Desire Type

## Awareness
Problem-aware. He knows his hip is stiff in the morning. He thinks it's just "getting old." He hasn't connected it to treatable joint inflammation that compounds overnight.

## Concept
The ad opens with the sound of an alarm. 5:30am. "I wake up 30 minutes before everyone else. Not because I'm a morning person. Because it takes me that long to be able to walk." The viewer who has morning stiffness feels personally called out. The belt enters as the thing he uses while the kettle boils. "15 minutes. Heat and vibration loosen the joint. By the time the coffee's ready, so am I."

## Angle
Morning stiffness. The first steps of every day shouldn't feel like the hardest.

# Brief 4: Lost Activities

## ICP
Australians 50-65 who have quietly stopped doing the things they love because of hip pain. They didn't announce it. They just stopped saying yes. Stopped gardening. Stopped walking the dog. Stopped visiting friends. Stopped playing with grandkids. The world got smaller and nobody noticed except them.

## POV
First person. Woman, 59. Used to garden every weekend. Used to walk the coastal track near her house. Used to get on the floor with her grandkids. Now she watches from the chair. She tells everyone she's "just tired."

## Summary of the Story
A woman who was always active has quietly withdrawn from life. She hasn't announced it. She hasn't made a dramatic statement. She just started saying "not today" more and more until "not today" became "not anymore." She finds the belt and starts wearing it after dinner. After a week, she walks to the end of the street. After two weeks, she's back in the garden. She didn't get a miracle. She got enough relief to say "yes" again.

## Type
Quiet loss / reclamation narrative

## Desire Type
Desire for participation. She doesn't want to be a spectator in her own life. She wants to say yes again.

## Awareness
Problem-aware leaning unaware. She knows she has hip pain. She doesn't connect the pain to the slow disappearance of her activities. She's normalised the shrinkage. The ad makes her see it.

## Concept
The ad opens with a list of things she used to do. "Used to garden every Saturday. Used to walk the dog after dinner. Used to get on the floor with the grandkids. Used to say yes." Then a pause. "When did I start saying 'not today'?" The viewer inventories their own losses. The belt enters not as a medical device but as a return ticket. "It gave me enough relief to say yes again. That's all I needed."

## Angle
Lost activities. The slow disappearance of the things that made life yours.

# Brief 5: Identity Threat

## ICP
Australians 50-60 who are younger than they feel. They're fit (or were). They don't see themselves as "old." But hip pain is making them feel old. They catch themselves moving like their parents did. They avoid stairs. They grunt getting out of a chair. The pain isn't just physical -- it's an identity crisis. "I feel like an old person" is one of the most common phrases in the VOC research.

## POV
First person. Man, 53. Still works full-time. Plays social tennis on Saturdays (or used to). His mates don't know how bad the hip is because he hides it. He takes 3 Nurofen before social events so nobody sees him limp.

## Summary of the Story
A man who's always been "the fit one" in his friend group is watching his body betray him. He hides the pain. He takes painkillers before seeing people. He stopped playing tennis but told everyone he's "taking a break." He doesn't want to be the bloke who hobbles. He finds the belt because he needs to feel like himself again, not because he's looking for a medical device.

## Type
Identity crisis / self-image preservation

## Desire Type
Desire to stay who he is. He doesn't want sympathy. He doesn't want to be "the one with the bad hip." He wants to keep being the guy who says "I'll be fine" and means it.

## Awareness
Problem-aware. He knows he has hip pain. He doesn't think of himself as someone who needs a therapy device. The positioning needs to match his self-image: practical, no-nonsense, evidence-based. Not medical. Not old.

## Concept
The ad opens with a mirror moment. He catches himself in the hallway mirror, mid-shuffle, and doesn't recognise the way he moves. "When did I start walking like my dad?" The ad isn't about pain reduction. It's about identity reclamation. The belt enters as the thing that gives him back his walk, his posture, his confidence. "I don't need to be 25 again. I just need to stop feeling 75."

## Angle
Identity threat. Hip pain doesn't just hurt your body. It changes who you see in the mirror.

## Current Belief
- "I'm not that bad. I just need to push through."
- "A therapy belt is for old people. I'm not there yet."
- "If I start using gadgets, I'm admitting something I'm not ready to admit."

## Moments

- Taking 3 Nurofen before a BBQ so nobody sees him limp
- Telling his mates he's "taking a break from tennis" when really his hip won't let him play

Relationship / intimacy restoration (implied, never explicit)

Unaware. She hasn't connected hip pain to relationship distance. She thinks the distance is emotional. It's physical. The pain is the wall between them and she doesn't see it.
