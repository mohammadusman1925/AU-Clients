If a surgeon has told you there's nothing else to try, this is worth two minutes.

I'm Sandra. I've been a theatre nurse for 38 years. I've handed surgeons the instruments for hundreds of shoulder procedures. I've watched women come back a few years later with the other side gone the same way. I've seen the trajectory.

So when my own shoulder started stiffening, I knew the road before anyone drew it for me.

"We'll start you on a cortisone injection."

I smiled. Took the referral.

And I never made the appointment.

Not because I'm difficult. Not because I think doctors are hiding something. But because I've seen what happens when people lean on those to manage something that's still tightening underneath, and I didn't want to go down that road.

Both roads led somewhere I didn't want to go. I was trying to find a third one.

For two years, I worked around it instead. I stopped using my right hand for anything above my head. I started stepping into my jacket rather than swinging it on. I moved the everyday things down a shelf, one at a time, over months, so it never looked like a decision. Panadol quietened it down and the stiffness stayed exactly where it was.

I told myself I was managing it. I was hiding it.

Then one morning in my own driveway, I reached across for the seatbelt the way I have since I was seventeen, and my shoulder just stopped.

Not pain first. A wall first. Then the pain, arriving half a second late like it had to catch up.

My daughter was in the passenger seat. She watched me twist my whole body and clip the belt in with my left hand. She didn't say anything.

That's the bit I couldn't get past. Not the pain. Her not saying anything.

So I finally went and got it looked at.

The surgeon was decent about it. He took the arm through its range and showed me how little movement was left. He said the tissue around the joint had thickened and locked it down so far that it could take years to loosen on its own, if it did at all. He wanted to go in and free it up. That is a real thing that happens.

He put me on the list.

Then something happened that I still think about.

Three weeks later I was running pre-admission clinic. That's the appointment where we work you up before the day. A man about my age was booked in for the same operation I'd assisted with more times than I can count.

He told me he was cancelling.

People cancel. Usually they're frightened. He wasn't frightened. He put his arm straight up over his head in front of me, held it there, and said he didn't need it any more.

"What did you do?"

He grinned. "You're not going to believe me."

"Try me."

"I stopped waiting and started treating it myself. A wrap, twenty minutes a day. About three months ago."

I looked at him. "You're telling me a wrap did that."

"I'm telling you I sleep on that side now. I can reach into the back seat. And I don't need the operation."

I drove home annoyed.

Then I was awake most of that night, and it wasn't my shoulder keeping me up. It was that a man I was supposed to be prepping for surgery knew something I didn't, and I'd spent 38 years standing in that room.

I've been a nurse for 38 years. I don't believe in miracle gadgets. I trust mechanisms.

So I did what I should have done two years earlier. I went and read.

It's newer than anything I'd been offered, and that's the honest reason it had never come up. Getting these wavelengths into something you can strap on and wear at home has only become possible in the last few years. Before that it was clinic equipment. Physios have it. So do the people who look after racehorses, and nobody is spending that kind of money on a horse for a placebo.

It had just never once come up in any conversation I'd had about my own shoulder.

Here's what I worked out, and it isn't complicated.

The shoulder isn't locking up because you're old. It's starved. The tissue around the joint has a poor blood supply at the best of times, and once it swells, the swelling squeezes the little supply it has. Less blood means it can't settle. Not settling means more swelling and more tightening.

It's a loop, and it feeds itself.

Now think about what gets offered for that. Anti-inflammatories quieten the alarm and don't touch the loop. Physio stretches the tissue, which is the right idea if the tissue isn't too inflamed to respond, and mine was. Cortisone settles it down for a while, then it comes back on schedule.

And the operation cuts the tight tissue loose. It frees the joint, but it doesn't restore the blood supply. That's why the other side so often goes the same way.

I'd been standing in that room for 38 years looking straight at the answer, and I never once asked the question.

I'll save you a step, because I tried something else first.

I bought a small handheld red light online. About forty dollars. It looked the part. Nothing changed, and now I understand why.

Most of the handhelds are red LEDs on their own. Red light at around 660 nanometres is good, but it works near the surface. The wavelength that reaches the deep tissue is near infrared, around 850, and the small ones don't include it. There's no warmth, so the vessels never open. And there's nothing holding it in place, so you end up holding it up with your good arm for ten minutes and hoping.

Same colour. Different job entirely.

What I ended up using is a wrap made for the shoulder that does four things at the same time, and the four together is the part that matters. It was built by a small group of engineers in Sydney, which I'll admit is what made me look twice, because after 38 years around theatre equipment I've got fairly firm opinions about who builds what.

Red light at 660 for the surface tissue, and near infrared at 850 to reach the deep tissue. Gentle heat, so the vessels open and the light lands on tissue that actually has blood moving through it. Vibration. And light compression, so it sits exactly where it's meant to instead of sliding off your shoulder.

Twenty minutes. I did it sitting in front of the news.

I'll tell you plainly why I bought it. A hundred and fifty dollars, with thirty days to send it back. I'd already spent more than that on heat packs, creams and a physio I'd stopped being honest with. This was the first money I'd spent that was aimed at the actual problem, and if nothing came of it I'd have it back.

Things came back in the order they'd left, which I wasn't expecting.

The jacket first. I swung it on and got halfway down the hall before I realised what I'd just done, and then I stood there like an idiot in my own hallway.

Then reaching into the back seat, instead of getting out and walking round the car.

Then the bra clasp, which I'd been doing in stages in front of the mirror for over a year and had never mentioned to a single person.

The seatbelt was last. Of course it was.

I saw the surgeon again six weeks later for my pre-op review. He took the arm through the range. Stopped. Took it through again.

Then he sat down and asked me what I'd been doing.

I told him. He listened properly, which I appreciated.

"Whatever this is, keep doing it."

Then he asked whether I wanted to stay on the list.

I've mentioned it since to a few patients I've sat with in clinic. Not everyone. The ones where I could see the same road I'd been on. Two of them have come back and told me about it. One rang the ward to tell me, which I thought was a bit much until she explained why.

I'm not anti-surgery. I've spent my whole working life in that room and I've watched it give people their arm back. If you need it, have it.

But if you're where I was, saying no to things and calling that a plan, I'd think about this.

Being careful isn't a plan. I was very good at being careful for two years, and every workaround I came up with was quietly letting it get worse. The shoulder wasn't wearing out. It was starved, and it was stuck, and nobody had aimed a single thing at that.

I got in the car this morning and reached across without planning it. It clicked. I was down the street before it occurred to me that something used to happen there.

My slot went to someone else. After 38 years looking at that list from the other side, that felt like the right way to leave it.
